\needspace{10\baselineskip}\subsection*{Overall assessment}

\rc{I recommend major revision. The claimed contributions (internal-camera proprioception, learned inverse kinematics, closed-loop soft-arm control, and redundancy resolution) have each been extensively addressed in prior work. [\dots] Several central claims also extend beyond the evidence presented. [\dots]}

We thank the reviewer for the detailed and constructive assessment. We agree that the contribution lies in the integration of distributed internal cameras, markerless at inference, with model-based and learned redundancy-aware control on a multi-section arm, and we have revised the manuscript to state this precisely and to align the claims with the evidence. In summary: (i) we added the suggested references, a comparison paragraph in the Introduction, and a comparison table in the SI; (ii) we state explicitly that the curvature reconstruction does not enter the control laws and explain the encoder offset in Algorithm~1; (iii) we narrowed the scalability claim to networks retrained with a transferred backbone, validated for one and three sections; (iv) we describe the multi-section curvature result as a model-constrained reconstruction and state its assumptions; (v) we added an offline ablation of the secondary objectives of both controllers; (vi) we quantified the coverage of the control evaluation and limited the claims on repeatability and disturbances; (vii) we documented the data split and its effect, and added a reproducibility table; and (viii) we revised the figures and captions as requested. \needspace{10\baselineskip}\subsection*{Major comments}

\needspace{8\baselineskip}\subsubsection*{Comment 1}

\rc{1. Clarify the role of visual sensing in closed-loop control. [\dots] Algorithm 1 also uses a PCC inverse calculation to initialize tendon lengths from section-tip positions. [\dots] the resulting estimate appears to be replaced by the motor-encoder measurement at the first control iteration, before it affects the control command. The authors should explain this initialization or remove it. The abstract, contribution list, Fig. 4c, and conclusion should clearly distinguish [\dots]}

We thank the reviewer for this careful reading. The reviewer is correct that the curvature reconstruction does not enter either control law: both controllers close the loop on the tip pose estimated from the camera images, the model-based controller additionally uses the motor encoders to evaluate the PCC Jacobian and the null-space term, and the curvature estimate is evaluated as a separate sensing capability. We now state this explicitly in the Abstract, the contribution list, Section~3.3, the caption of Fig.~4c (which now shows the encoder path), and the Conclusion.

The initial PCC estimate in Algorithm~1 is not discarded. The tendon lengths $\mathbf{l}_0$ obtained from the camera-estimated section-tip positions through the inverse PCC map are used once, before the loop, to set the encoder offset $\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{off}} = \mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{enc}} - \mathbf{l}_0$, so that the encoder readings inside the loop are expressed in the PCC tendon-length frame used by $\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{l})$ and by the null-space term $\mathbf{N}(\bar{\mathbf{l}} - \mathbf{l})$. Algorithm~1 and Section~3.3.2 now show this step explicitly.

\needspace{8\baselineskip}\subsubsection*{Comment 2}

\rc{2. Establish originality claims through direct comparison with prior work. [\dots] The authors should explain what each close prior study established and identify the specific capability added here. The claim that no prior framework combines comparable sensing with closed-loop null-space control is too broad without this analysis.}

We thank the reviewer for these references. We added Hofer et al., Thuruthel et al., Han et al., and Lu et al.\ (Yoo et al.\ was already cited as Ref.~[15]) and a paragraph in the Introduction that states what each study established, together with a comparison table in the SI (Table~S10). Since the systems differ in actuation, size, and task, the table compares design choices rather than accuracy: the sensing, the part of the arm that is sensed, the estimated quantities, whether the loop is closed on the estimate, and how the actuation redundancy is used. Each prior system covers part of these choices, and ours is the only one that combines them. Hofer et al.\ obtain a higher orientation accuracy (about $1^\circ$ at 30~Hz) than our single-section estimate ($3.11^\circ$ at about 8~Hz), on a two-angle spherical joint with an internal painted pattern and illumination; our system estimates the full 6-DoF tip pose of an extensible section without internal markers and is applied to three sections. Thuruthel et al.\ learned closed-loop inverse kinematics with end-effector feedback, Han et al.\ and Yoo et al.\ estimate shape from internal cameras without closing a control loop, and Lu et al.\ servo the shape with fiber Bragg grating sensing. The capability we add is the combination of distributed internal cameras on a multi-section arm with closed-loop control that explicitly exploits the actuation redundancy, through model-based null-space control and through a learned inverse kinematics with a secondary objective. We narrowed the novelty statement in the Introduction and the contribution list to this combination.

\needspace{8\baselineskip}\subsubsection*{Comment 3}

\rc{3. Substantiate or narrow the scalability claim. The estimator is not invariant to the number of arm sections. [\dots] The statement that only the first layer must be adjusted also conflicts with Fig. 3 because the regression head changes as well.}

We agree. The input layer and the regression head both depend on the number of sections, and separate networks were trained for the one- and three-section arms. We corrected the statement in Section~3.1 and rephrased the Introduction, the contribution list, and the Conclusion accordingly: the backbone is transferred and the network is retrained, and only $n = 1$ and $n = 3$ were validated. We also removed the word ``scalable'' where it implied that no retraining is needed.

\needspace{8\baselineskip}\subsubsection*{Comment 4}

\rc{4. Strengthen the ground truth for full-body reconstruction. [\dots] Fig. S2b does not provide sufficient validation of N\_C [\dots] Otherwise, the result should be described as model-constrained reconstruction, with its assumptions stated explicitly, including planar bending within each section, no torsion, and no arc-dependent bending direction.}

We agree. For the multi-section arm, inter-section marker occlusion prevented tracking all backbone markers during data collection, and the curvature labels were obtained from the motion-captured section-tip poses through $N_C$. We therefore now describe the multi-section result as a model-constrained reconstruction and state its assumptions explicitly in Section~2.2 and Section~4.2.2: planar bending within each section, no torsion, and a bending direction that is constant along each section. The errors in Fig.~7b quantify the agreement with these model-constrained labels. For Fig.~S2b, we now report the number of validation configurations (20, posed manually with all subsection markers visible and recorded in a separate session), that the error bars are one standard deviation across configurations, and that these configurations are independent of the data used to train $N_C$ (single-section dataset) and the three-section curvature estimator. We also report the error of $N_C$ on held-out single-section test data ($N = 500$): 0.38--0.43~mm at $L/3$, 1.20--1.37~mm at $2L/3$, and 2.49--2.94~mm at $L$ for the three curvature models (SI Section~4.2).

\needspace{8\baselineskip}\subsubsection*{Comment 5}

\rc{5. Correct the curvature-coordinate inconsistency. [\dots] The paper should consistently use either normalized arc length $u = x/L$ or dimensional arc length $x \in [0, L]$, and revise Eqs. 4--6, SI Eqs. S3--S9, the translation integral, nomenclature, and coefficient units accordingly.}

We thank the reviewer. The implementation and the SI equations use the dimensional arc length $x \in [0, L]$. We corrected the main text accordingly (Eqs.~4--6, the translation integral without the factor $L$, and the evaluation points $x = L/3, 2L/3, L$), the variable of SI Eqs.~S3--S4 and the initialization sentence in SI Section~2.2, and the nomenclature in Table~S1, which now gives the units of $c_0$, $c_1$, and $c_2$ (mm$^{-1}$, mm$^{-2}$, mm$^{-3}$).

\needspace{8\baselineskip}\subsubsection*{Comment 6}

\rc{6. Quantitatively evaluate the secondary objective. [\dots] The objective should therefore might described as actuator-range centering unless effort is measured. For both controllers, an ablation with the secondary objective disabled using identical targets would be helpful. [\dots]}

We agree that $H(\mathbf{l})$ centers the tendon lengths in their actuation range and does not measure effort, which was not recorded. We now describe it as actuator-range centering and removed the statements about effort (Introduction, Section~3.3.2, Conclusion). To isolate its effect without new hardware experiments, we performed offline ablations on identical targets for both controllers (new SI Section~5.3, Fig.~S8, and Table~S13). For the model-based controller, replaying the control law on the PCC model for the 20 evaluation targets gives a final position error of $5.25 \pm 2.23$~mm with and $5.84 \pm 1.85$~mm without the null-space term, while the RMS deviation of the tendon lengths from $\bar{\mathbf{l}}$ decreases from $12.00 \pm 3.18$~mm to $7.48 \pm 1.96$~mm ($H(\mathbf{l})$: $693 \pm 396$ vs.\ $269 \pm 135$). For the data-driven controller, we retrained the inverse-kinematics network with and without the secondary term ($\zeta = 0.01$ and $\zeta = 0$, same data, split, and seed) and evaluated it through the frozen forward network on the same targets: the secondary term reduces $H(\mathbf{l})$ from $487 \pm 257$ to $371 \pm 166$ (RMS tendon deviation from 10.1 to 8.8~mm), at the cost of a small increase of the position error predicted by the forward network ($5.1 \pm 2.1$ vs.\ $4.6 \pm 2.3$~mm). Unlike the null-space projection, the penalty in the training loss trades some primary-task accuracy for range centering, which we now state in Section~3.3.2. These results are simulated through the respective models and are reported as such.

\needspace{8\baselineskip}\subsubsection*{Comment 7}

\rc{7. Expand and better characterize the control evaluation. [\dots] The authors should report the target coordinates, associated bending and compression ranges, and the fraction of the sampled workspace represented by the evaluation set. Each of the 20 targets is visited only once. [\dots] Either additional load magnitudes and directions should be tested or the claims should be limited.}

We thank the reviewer. We replaced the bending range in Section~3.2 with the measured one (single section: maximum $57^\circ$, 95th percentile $36^\circ$; multi-section 95th percentiles of $30^\circ$, $32^\circ$, and $34^\circ$), and we added the coordinates of all targets with their bending angle and length change (Tables~S8 and S9) and the coverage of the evaluation set (SI Section~6.3 and Fig.~S9). The single-section targets and trajectories reach $33.7^\circ$ and $40.9^\circ$, close to the 95th percentile of the training data ($35.6^\circ$); the 20 targets span 9.2\% (single) and 16.4\% (multi) of the convex hull of the sampled positions. The length changes of the targets are $-20.5$ to $-9.7$~mm (single section; training data, 5th to 95th percentile: $-24.4$ to $-7.0$~mm) and $-18.5$ to $-12.7$~mm per section (multi-section); we agree that this is a limited range of compression and now state it in SI Section~6.3. We revised the text to state that the reported standard deviations describe the variation across targets visited once, not repeatability, and that trajectory waypoints are serially correlated. The disturbance experiment is now described as the compensation of a single constant 50~g off-center load rather than general robustness. Repeated trials, non-planar and boundary trajectories, larger length changes, and further load conditions are listed as limitations and future work in Section~5.

\needspace{8\baselineskip}\subsubsection*{Comment 8}

\rc{8. Establish independence of training and evaluation data. [\dots] The authors should state: how samples were randomized; whether test data were separated by session or configuration neighborhood; whether controller targets and trajectories were excluded from training; and whether experiments span different days, camera settings, coverings, or robot builds.}

We added this information to Section~3.1.1 and SI Section~4.1. All samples of each dataset were pooled and randomly permuted with a fixed seed before the 85/10/5 division; the split was not blocked by session or by configuration neighborhood. Because each commanded configuration of the multi-section arm was held for about 3~s and recorded at 1~Hz, some test samples share their configuration with training samples. To quantify the effect, we evaluated the test error with and without these samples: for the multi-section arm, the tip position errors become 4.60, 14.72, and 27.99~mm instead of 4.44, 14.01, and 26.45~mm (Table~S14), and for the single-section arm, samples close to a training configuration are not more accurate than the others (4.08 vs.\ 3.86~mm). Session-blocked evaluation is listed as a limitation. The control targets were selected among the sampled workspace configurations and were therefore not excluded from training; the closed-loop experiments use new camera images, and the trajectories are not part of the datasets. The single-section data were collected in one day and the multi-section data on two days, with the same camera settings and one build of each arm \textbf{[CONFIRM]}; the two arms use different coverings (corrugated silicone and stretchable fabric, Section~2.1).

\needspace{8\baselineskip}\subsubsection*{Comment 16 (pages 5--7)}

\rc{Explain how angular sine/cosine outputs are converted back to angles and how losses for position, orientation, length, and curvature are balanced after normalization.}

The networks regress the angles directly; the sine and cosine of the predicted and target angles are only used inside the loss to remove the $2\pi$ discontinuity, so no conversion back to angles is needed. Positions, lengths, and curvature coefficients are standardized with the mean and standard deviation of the training set, and all loss terms have equal weight. This is now stated in SI Section~3.1 and Table~S11.

\needspace{8\baselineskip}\subsubsection*{Comment 17 (pages 5--7)}

\rc{Clarify the negative sign in Eq. S11 and clarify how the highlighted pixels are the regions used for prediction.}

In Eq.~S11 the Grad-CAM score is the negative loss, so positive weights correspond to feature-map channels whose activation reduces the prediction loss; after the ReLU of Eq.~S12, the heatmap retains only these regions. The maps are normalized to $[0, 1]$ per image and shown without thresholding, so the color scale in Fig.~4a shows this relative contribution. We clarified both points in SI Section~3.2 and in the caption of Fig.~4.

\needspace{8\baselineskip}\subsubsection*{Comment 19 (pages 5--7)}

\rc{Use consistent Jacobian notation. J\_t2l, J\_l2p, and their learned representations currently alternate between a forward Jacobian, an inverse Jacobian, and a derivative of an inverse network. [\dots]}

We now use a single notation, defined in Section~3.3: $\mathbf{J}_{a \to b} = \partial b / \partial a$. The analytical PCC Jacobians are forward Jacobians ($\mathbf{J}_{l \to t}$, $\mathbf{J}_{l \to p}$) with damped pseudoinverse $\mathbf{J}^{\#}$, and the Jacobians of the learned inverse kinematics are denoted with a hat ($\hat{\mathbf{J}}_{t \to l}$, $\hat{\mathbf{J}}_{p \to l}$). The notation is used consistently in Eqs.~7--10, Algorithms~1--2, Fig.~S3, and Table~S2.
